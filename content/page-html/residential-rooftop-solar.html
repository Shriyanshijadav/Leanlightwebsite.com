What residential rooftop solar involves

Residential rooftop solar is a set of solar panels on the roof of a home, wired through an inverter to the home's electricity connection, so the household uses its own solar power first and buys less from the grid.

For a home in India it involves five things: a site survey and design, the equipment itself, installation, a net-metering agreement with the local distribution company, and, for eligible households, a subsidy claim under PM Surya Ghar. Surplus daytime units are exported through a bidirectional meter and credited against what the home imports at night. A system lasts 25 years or more, with the inverter usually replaced once in that time.

Is this right for you?

Solar pays back well on most Indian homes, but not all. These are the conditions we check, and the cases where we advise against it.

You are a good fit if

  • You own the home, or the society has passed a resolution for common-area solar
  • Your bill is above roughly ₹1,500 a month, so the saving outweighs the fixed cost
  • You have about 100 sq ft of shadow-free roof for each kW, on an RCC slab or a sound metal roof
  • Your sanctioned load is at least the size of the system you want, or you are willing to raise it
  • The electricity connection is in your name and no solar subsidy has been claimed on it

It may not suit you if

  • The roof is shaded for most of the day by a taller building, trees or a water tank you cannot move
  • You rent, or plan to sell within two or three years
  • The roof needs waterproofing or structural repair first: do that before the panels go on
  • Your bill is very low, where payback stretches beyond the usual range

Housing societies buying solar for lifts, pumps and common lighting have different approvals and a different subsidy rate. Their process is on the housing society solar page.

System options

Four configurations. The right one depends on how reliable your grid supply is.

No battery

On-grid

Connected to the grid with no battery. Solar runs the home by day, surplus is exported through the net meter and the grid supplies the night. Lowest cost, fastest payback and fully eligible for subsidy. It switches off during a power cut. The right choice wherever supply is reliable.

No grid connection

Off-grid

Independent of the grid. Panels charge a battery bank through the day and the battery runs the home at night. Built for farmhouses and sites with no connection or only a few hours of supply. It costs the most, needs careful sizing and is not eligible for the central subsidy.

Off-grid solar system →
Grid plus battery

Hybrid

Grid-connected like an on-grid system, with a battery that takes over when the grid fails. Essential loads keep running through outages and you still export surplus. The subsidy applies to the solar capacity but not to the battery. Suited to areas with frequent or long power cuts.

Add-on

Battery storage

A lithium battery added to a new or existing solar system, sized in kWh for the loads you want backed up and the hours you need them. It can be fitted when you install solar or retrofitted later. The decision is mostly about backup, because storage rarely pays back on bill savings alone.

How to read the table

Cost per kW falls as systems grow, but the subsidy stops rising at 3 kW, so the subsidised share is largest on small systems. Size for your consumption, not for the subsidy: a system that generates more than you use earns little on the surplus in most states.

Payback is fastest for homes in the higher tariff slabs, because solar units displace your most expensive grid units first. A home paying ₹8 or more a unit usually recovers the cost within four years. Subsidy, incentives and approvals

Homes on a domestic connection qualify for the central subsidy under PM Surya Ghar: ₹30,000 per kW for the first 2 kW, ₹18,000 for the third and a ₹78,000 ceiling. Group housing societies receive ₹18,000 per kW for common facilities, up to 500 kW. Some states add their own top-up. Eligibility, documents and the claim process are covered step by step in the PM Surya Ghar subsidy guide.

Every grid-connected home system also needs net metering. Your DISCOM checks technical feasibility against the local transformer, signs a net-metering agreement and replaces your meter with a bidirectional one. Exported units are then credited against imported units on each bill. Rules on system size relative to sanctioned load, and on how surplus is settled at year end, differ by state. We make both applications for you.

Residential solar questions

What size solar system does a home need?

Most Indian homes need 2 to 5 kW. Divide your average monthly units by 120 to estimate the kW required, since each kW generates about 120 units a month. The solar system sizes page matches capacity to typical bills.

How much will my electricity bill fall?

A correctly sized system offsets 70 to 90 percent of the units you buy. Fixed charges and any units used beyond what you generate remain. Enter your bill in the solar savings calculator for a figure.

How long does a home installation take?

Installation takes one to three days. Allow four to eight weeks from site visit to a working net meter, because DISCOM approvals take the longest. We file those applications as soon as you confirm the order.

Can a housing society get the subsidy?

Yes. Group housing societies and resident welfare associations receive ₹18,000 per kW for common-area systems up to 500 kW. The PM Surya Ghar subsidy guide explains what counts as a common facility.

Will solar work on a flat I own in an apartment building?

Only if you have rights to a part of the terrace and the society agrees. In most apartment buildings the practical route is a common-area system owned by the society. The on-grid solar system page explains how that is metered.

Should I add a battery?

Add one if power cuts are frequent where you live. A battery provides backup but adds cost and does not attract subsidy. See solar battery storage for sizing and cost.

Do I need permission from anyone?

You need DISCOM feasibility approval and a net-metering agreement, which we obtain. Societies need a general-body resolution. No municipal building permission is normally required for a rooftop system, though To confirm: state-specific exceptions.
